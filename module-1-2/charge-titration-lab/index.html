Rules of thumb

Objective 1. Classify each ionizable group as weakly acidic or weakly basic, and use pH versus pKa to assign its charge.

Where is the pH relative to the pKa?

Weakly acidic groups go from HA (0) to A⁻ (−1). Weakly basic groups go from HA⁺ (+1) to A (0, N with a lone pair). The pKa is the dividing line: below it the acid form predominates, above it the base form does.

Net charge of a polypeptide

Objective 3. Calculate the net charge on a polypeptide at a given pH, using the shifted pKas of the free N- and C-termini.

In a polypeptide, only the free N-terminus (left), the free C-terminus (right) and ionizable side chains count. The termini sit next to amides now, so their pKas shift: N-terminal α-NH₃⁺ 9.5 → 8.5 and C-terminal α-COOH 2.5 → 3.5.

Titration curves

Objective 4. Follow the net charge through an amino acid titration and identify an amino acid or peptide from its curve.

Walk through a titration

We start at very low pH, where everything is protonated, and add strong base. Groups are titrated from lowest pKa to highest, so the α-COOH always goes first. Each half-equivalent of OH⁻ lowers the net charge by ½. The total equivalents equal the number of weakly acidic and basic groups.

Mystery curve

Read the pH at each half-equivalence point (0.5, 1.5, 2.5 …) to get the pKas. The first is always the α-COOH and one is always the α-NH₃⁺; whatever is left belongs to a side chain.

Isoelectric point (pI)

Objective 5. Calculate the pI of any of the 20 amino acids and of a small polypeptide.

The zero sits between +1 and −1

The pI is the pH where net charge is 0. That 0 form always sits between the +1 and −1 forms, so the pI is the average of the two pKas on either side of it.
